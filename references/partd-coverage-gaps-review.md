# Weighting for coverage loss goes beyond USRDS precedent

*Section numbers refer to the analysis plan as restructured on 4 November 2026. The Santos pipeline the review discusses is now in `references/implementation-notes.md`.*

**Bottom line.** In the notes reviewed, no published kidney transplant study using United States Renal Data System (USRDS) data treated loss of Medicare or Part D coverage as anything other than ordinary censoring. None used inverse probability of censoring weights (IPCW) for coverage loss. In the wider USRDS dialysis literature, only one study did: Shen and colleagues in 2015. They weighted for loss of Parts A and B in the intention-to-treat analysis, and for loss of Part D or the low-income subsidy (LIS) in the as-treated analysis ([AJKD 2015](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.ajkd.2015.05.019)). The common pattern has three parts. Studies require continuous Medicare Parts A, B and D, with Medicare as primary payer, for 6 to 12 months before baseline. They allow no stated gap in enrollment. Then they censor at "loss of Medicare primary payer status" without weights. Two newer problems are handled poorly or not at all. The first is the 2021 shift into Medicare Advantage (MA) after the 21st Century Cures Act. One recent target trial emulation found that 83% of its coverage-loss censoring happened in 2021, and it handled this by cutting follow-up at the end of 2020 as a sensitivity analysis ([Kidney Int Rep 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ekir.2026.106618)). The second is the transplant-specific end of Medicare 36 months after transplant. Older transplant studies dealt with this by keeping all analysis inside the first three years, or by following patients "until loss of Medicare." For the planned trial of methenamine versus prophylactic-dose antibiotics versus neither, the draft plan already goes beyond precedent: it weights for loss of observation inside the same clone-censor-weight framework. The main changes it needs are elsewhere. It should be explicit about the 36-month cliff and the 2021 shift. It should drop the positive-patient-pay filter. It should check for veterans whose fills may happen outside Medicare. And it should report how many recipients stay observable over time, because no published source does this.

**How to read this report.** Abbreviations are defined at first use. Statements followed by a source link report what that source says. Statements marked **Inference** are my reasoning from the sources, not something a source says. Items marked **[Unverified]** could not be confirmed against a primary source in the research notes and should be checked before citing. Evidence from general Medicare or commercial insurance is labeled **[General Medicare]** or **[Commercial]** and is used only where USRDS evidence is missing.

## Enrollment rules: everyone requires A, B and D, but nobody uses contract prefixes

USRDS studies define Part D coverage with the monthly Part D enrollment indicators. They almost always pair it with the payer history file's MPAB code, which means "Medicare FFS primary pay, for both Part A and Part B." The USRDS Researcher's Guide says Medicare claims "are complete for MPP enrollees only." It describes monthly indicators for Part D, LIS, dual Medicare-Medicaid eligibility and the Retiree Drug Subsidy (RDS), but it gives **no recommended Part D enrollment rule** ([USRDS Researcher's Guide 2025](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/-/media/Files/USRDS/For-Researchers/2025/USRDS_Researcher_Guide_2025.pdf)). No dialysis or transplant methods section in the notes used contract-ID prefixes (H, R, S, E) to define coverage. The Santos pipeline the plan inherits (now in the implementation notes) is therefore more detailed than anything in the published methods. Santos itself described its rule only as excluding people with "no Medicare Part A, B or D coverage" ([Santos 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4818663/)).

Requiring MPAB already removes most MA prescription drug plan (MA-PD) months in practice, because an MA payer shows as "HMO" in payer history ([USRDS Researcher's Guide 2025](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/-/media/Files/USRDS/For-Researchers/2025/USRDS_Researcher_Guide_2025.pdf)). **Inference:** this is why few papers bother to separate stand-alone prescription drug plans (PDPs) from MA-PD plans. **[Unverified]** In general CMS practice, contracts beginning with H and R are MA or similar plans, S contracts are stand-alone PDPs, and E contracts are employer direct contracts. If that holds, the Santos pipeline's rule that counts H, R, S and E as enrolled does not match §3.1's wording of "stand-alone Part D." The MPAB requirement should screen out most H and R months. However, any month where payer history and the Part D contract disagree (for example, a cost plan or a mid-month change) would need a rule. The prefix meanings and the PDENROL variable names should be checked against Appendix B of the Researcher's Guide, which the research notes did not read.

Two other details matter. The Researcher's Guide warns that Part D data from "the first few months of 2006 ... may be incomplete" ([USRDS Researcher's Guide 2025](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/-/media/Files/USRDS/For-Researchers/2025/USRDS_Researcher_Guide_2025.pdf)). Also, RDS beneficiaries have employer drug coverage subsidized by Medicare and have no Part D event records at all. **Inference:** the plan's §3.2 L3 is right to treat them as not observable rather than as non-users.

## Lookbacks run 6 to 12 months, and nobody states a gap tolerance

Lookback windows in USRDS Part D studies range from 90 days to more than 3 years. Six months is common in the Stanford and Baylor group's work and in the Chronic Disease Research Group's work, and 12 months is also common ([Shen 2015](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.ajkd.2015.05.019); [Siontis 2018](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.118.035418)). Case-control studies needed more than 3 years of Part D to measure long-term exposure ([Vangala 2020](https://doi.org/10.1681/ASN.2019090904)). Platt and colleagues tested a 90-day lookback against 180 days and found similar results ([AJKD 2023](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.ajkd.2023.05.016)). Some groups also required at least one fill during the lookback, as proof that the drug benefit was actually used, not just held ([Shen 2015](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.ajkd.2015.05.019)).

**Not one USRDS dialysis or transplant paper in the notes stated any tolerance for an enrollment gap.** All used "continuous" or "uninterrupted" coverage. Ishida excluded anyone with intermittent coverage outright ([JASN 2018](https://doi.org/10.1681/ASN.2018010096)). The only gap rules that appear concern drug supply. For example, Santos summed days' supply while refill gaps were 15 days or less ([Santos 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4818663/)). **Inference:** the Santos pipeline's rule that restarts a span only after two consecutive non-enrolled months allows a one-month enrollment gap. That is more generous than any published precedent. It is defensible for administrative blips, but a month with no Part D enrollment is a month in which a methenamine or antibiotic fill cannot be seen. In a clone-censor-weight design, treatment status in that month drives whether a clone is censored, so the gap month should not count as observed time for judging protocol deviation.

The plan's 365-day continuous lookback (§3.1) is on the long end of the usual range. That is reasonable for measuring past antibiotic use and earlier UTI episodes. However, the time zero sits at least 180 days after transplant, so the lookback reaches back before the transplant for some patients. Pre-transplant months often fall under the dialysis entitlement, or under the Medicare Secondary Payer period. **Inference:** this will drop some recipients whose coverage became primary only around transplant. The notes contain no precedent for how a post-transplant study should handle a lookback that spans the transplant date.

## Censoring at coverage loss is routine, but weighting for it is rare

The default in USRDS target trial emulations is to censor at "loss of Medicare primary payer status" and assume the censoring is not informative. Le 2026, Le 2024 and Platt 2023 all did this. Where they used weights, the weights addressed drug discontinuation or non-adherence, not coverage loss ([Le 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ekir.2026.106618); [Le 2024](https://doi.org/10.1159/000538253); [Platt 2023](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.ajkd.2023.05.016)). Shen 2015 is the only confirmed USRDS exception. It fit a logistic model for censoring and multiplied stabilized, trimmed censoring weights by the treatment weights. However, it used only baseline covariates, not time-varying predictors of coverage loss ([AJKD 2015](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.ajkd.2015.05.019)). Among transplant studies, Santos 2016 censored at end of A, B or D without weights ([Santos 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4818663/)). Several self-described USRDS target trial emulations from 2025–2026, including a clone-censor-weight study by Roetker and colleagues, could only be read as abstracts. Their handling of coverage loss is **[Unverified]** ([Roetker 2026](https://doi.org/10.1681/ASN.0000001293); [Winkelmayer 2026](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.ajkd.2026.08.009)).

A useful pattern runs through the dialysis work. Outcomes that need claims are censored at loss of Parts A and B. Exposure or per-protocol follow-up is censored at loss of Part D, and sometimes at loss of LIS. Death, which the registry records regardless of payer, is often followed to study end ([Chang 2014](https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.114.001356); [Shen 2015](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.ajkd.2015.05.019)). Abbott 2018 used the same idea in transplant recipients, following death and graft loss past the exposure year ([Abbott 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5962376/)). **Inference:** the planned primary outcome, recurrent treated UTI, needs both Part A/B claims and Part D fills, so it must be censored at loss of either. Secondary outcomes from the registry, such as death and graft loss, could be followed further.

There is evidence that coverage loss is not random, but almost none of it comes from ESRD. In commercial claims, disenrollment ran from 22% to 40% a year. It was predicted by age, frailty, hospital and emergency visits, and certain drugs. The prediction model was weak (C-statistic about 0.62), and the authors recommended IPCW ([Butler 2019, Commercial](https://doi.org/10.1002/pds.4750)). The notes found **no published model of what predicts Part D or MPP loss in USRDS patients**. Methods writing also warns that requiring enrollment after the start of follow-up creates immortal-time and selection bias ([Ramachandran 2025, General](https://doi.org/10.18553/jmcp.2025.31.12.1304)). **Inference:** the plan's §8.2 approach, censoring at loss of eligible observation and weighting with pooled logistic IPCW alongside protocol-deviation censoring, goes beyond every USRDS precedent found. It is the right direction. Its value depends on including time-varying predictors of coverage loss, which the next two sections identify.

## The 2021 Medicare Advantage shift concentrates censoring in one year

Starting in 2021, the 21st Century Cures Act let people with kidney failure join MA plans. The MA share among ESRD beneficiaries rose from 24.8% in December 2020 to 37.4% in December 2021. In 2021, 17.6% of prevalent traditional Medicare ESRD beneficiaries switched. Switchers were more often Black (by 19.5 percentage points) and more often dual-eligible (by 20.8 points) ([Nguyen 2023](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.1426)). The MA share passed 43% by December 2022 ([Nguyen 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.32772)) and was described as more than 50% in 2024 ([ASN 2026](https://doi.org/10.1681/ASN.0000001200)). USRDS now receives MA claims but releases only FFS claims to researchers ([USRDS Researcher's Guide 2025](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/-/media/Files/USRDS/For-Researchers/2025/USRDS_Researcher_Guide_2025.pdf)).

The consequence for a cohort that requires MPAB is direct. In Le 2026, 4,658 patients were censored for loss of MPP in the intention-to-treat analysis, and **83% of those censorings occurred in 2021**. The authors' response was a sensitivity analysis that ended follow-up on 31 December 2020 ([Kidney Int Rep 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ekir.2026.106618)). After the Cures Act, MA enrollees with kidney failure were described as younger, less sick and more often low-income ([Cai 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-6773.70150)). The switch was also more common at chain-owned dialysis facilities ([Kim 2025](https://doi.org/10.1377/hlthaff.2025.00361)). MA enrollees with kidney failure also had lower transplant (adjusted rate ratio 0.71) and waitlisting rates ([Shah 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2026.14391)).

**Inference:** these studies concern dialysis patients and access to transplant, not recipients who already have a functioning graft. The research notes contain no data on how many transplant recipients switched to MA after 2021. Even so, the same law applies to recipients who still hold ESRD-based Medicare. A cohort that runs past 2020 should expect a burst of censoring in early 2021 that is linked to race, dual status, region and calendar time. The censoring model can include all of these. Two points follow. Calendar year (or a post-2020 indicator) belongs in the censoring model. And a December 2020 cutoff is the closest thing to precedent, so it should be reported.

## The 36-month rule decides who stays observable, and nobody has charted it

For recipients who qualify for Medicare only because of kidney failure, Medicare ends 36 months after a successful transplant. Recipients entitled through age or disability keep it ([CRS R43154](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20130716_R43154_0658a54cfcea840a85d6c00e6e4bfd42fdb6aed8.html)). Coverage ends "on the last day of the 36th month" ([GAO-07-1117](https://www.gao.gov/assets/a267349.html)). For people with employer group coverage, Medicare is the secondary payer for the first 30 months of ESRD eligibility. These people appear in USRDS but their claims are incomplete ([CRS R43154](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20130716_R43154_0658a54cfcea840a85d6c00e6e4bfd42fdb6aed8.html)).

Published transplant work handles the 36-month rule in three ways. Some studies keep all outcome measurement inside the first three years ([Gill 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19459800/); [Dharnidharka 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17699393/); [Mehrotra 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22226565/)). Some follow patients "until loss of Medicare" ([Lentine 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16183428/); [Santos 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4818663/)). Policy studies use the group that keeps lifetime coverage. Woodward defined likely lifetime eligibility as age 62 or older at transplant (so 65 by month 36) or "unemployed due to disability." Together these two groups made up 55% of transplants in 2002 ([Woodward 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3189683/)).

The best cross-sectional figures come from secondary summaries. Among 142,923 recipients under 65 with a functioning graft in 2010, about 41% were not Medicare-eligible ([CRS R43154](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20130716_R43154_0658a54cfcea840a85d6c00e6e4bfd42fdb6aed8.html)). These figures were not checked against the original USRDS table. **The notes found no published curve showing the share of recipients who keep A+B+D with Medicare primary at months 12, 24, 36 and beyond.** Researchers would have to build it from the USRDS payer history file.

**Inference:** this matters more for the planned trial than for most earlier work. Time zero comes after a second or third treated UTI, at least 180 days after transplant, and follow-up lasts 12 months (§7.4). Any recipient whose time zero falls after month 24, and who has ESRD-only entitlement, will be censored at month 36 with certainty. Censoring that is fully determined by a known rule is not something IPCW can fix. Weights for that group would be infinite, because no one like them stays observed. In practice, the estimate for late time zeros will describe only recipients with lifetime Medicare. The plan should say so, rather than imply the weights restore the full population. The cleanest choices are to cap eligible time zeros at month 24 for the main analysis, or to define the target population as recipients likely to keep Medicare (the Woodward proxy), or both, with the other as a sensitivity analysis.

The 2023 Part B immunosuppressive drug benefit (Part B-ID) does not change this. Under it, Part B pays "only" for immunosuppressive drugs, with no other Medicare items or services, and enrollees may not have other coverage such as employer plans or Medicaid ([CMS fact sheet](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/part-b-immunosuppressive-drug-coverage.pdf)). Only 104 people were enrolled as of February 2024 ([GAO-24-107230](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-107230)). **Inference:** a Part B-ID month will show immunosuppressant claims but no UTI diagnoses, antibiotic fills or methenamine fills, so it must not count as observable time. **[Unverified]** How USRDS or CMS enrollment files flag Part B-ID months (for example, a separate entitlement code) needs to be checked in the Researcher's Guide for 2023 and later files.

## Out-of-plan fills hit the cheapest drugs and may differ by arm

No study in the notes measured Part D capture of antibiotics, of methenamine, or in kidney failure or transplant patients. The evidence comes from general Medicare and commercial data. The strongest Part D study linked self-reported drug use to Part D claims in 2009. Male veterans were 11% less likely to have matching records, which points to fills through the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA). In contrast, drugs on generic discount lists were slightly *more* likely to match ([Zhou 2015, General Medicare](https://doi.org/10.1097/MLR.0000000000000341)). A survey study classified 53.5% of older Medicare drug users as users of low-cost generic programs. Those with Medicaid or VA or Tricare coverage had less than half the odds of using them ([Pauly 2016, General Medicare](https://doi.org/10.18553/jmcp.2016.22.6.741)). This figure is an upper bound, because it was inferred from prices rather than from missing claims. In commercial data, 20.1% of statin users had at least one missing claim, mostly because another payer paid (66.3%) or cash or a coupon was used (18.7%) ([Wade 2017, Commercial](https://doi.org/10.18553/jmcp.2017.23.9.936)).

USRDS researchers addressed this in three ways. Some restricted to LIS enrollees, who face little cost-sharing. Chang 2014 said LIS allows "observation of medication fill patterns without interruption" through the coverage gap ([JAHA 2014](https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.114.001356)). Others used an LIS-only sensitivity analysis, because LIS enrollees "have no incentive to obtain medications outside" Part D ([Yusuf 2014](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.ajkd.2013.11.015)). The Assimon and Flythe antibiotic studies used active comparators and a negative control outcome ([Assimon 2022](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamacardio.2021.4234)). No USRDS study used multiple imputation or formal bias analysis for missing fills. LIS restriction costs more in transplant than in dialysis. In 2015, 50.7% of transplant Part D enrollees had LIS, compared with about two-thirds or more of dialysis enrollees ([USRDS ADR 2017](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/-/media/Files/USRDS/2017--Previous-ADR/ESRD/v2_c10_partd_17.pdf); [USRDS ADR 2018](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/-/media/Files/USRDS/2018-Previous-ADR/Volume-2/Chapter/v2_c10_esrd_drugs_18_usrds.pdf)). The notes flag one conflict: one 2007 dialysis sample reported only 20.4% LIS ([Park 2014](https://doi.org/10.18553/jmcp.2014.20.8.862)), against 76% in another ([Frankenfield 2012](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.ajkd.2011.10.047)). The difference probably comes from different inclusion rules, but this was not examined.

**Inference:** in this trial, missing fills do more than misclassify exposure. Eligibility rests on "treated" UTI episodes, so a cash-paid nitrofurantoin or TMP-SMX (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) course can stop a patient from entering the trial. It can also hide prophylaxis in the "neither" arm. **[Unverified]** Methenamine is probably not on most $4 lists while the comparator antibiotics often are. If so, capture loss may differ between arms, and an active-comparator design would not cancel it. TMP-SMX for *Pneumocystis* (PJP) prophylaxis is a Part D drug and faces the same capture risk. This bears on the open PJP handling decision.

## Immunosuppressants sit in Part B, everything else in Part D

When Medicare paid for the transplant, Part B covers immunosuppressants, and Part D "will not provide immunosuppressive drug coverage" for these beneficiaries ([CRS R43154](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20130716_R43154_0658a54cfcea840a85d6c00e6e4bfd42fdb6aed8.html)). Part B claims lack National Drug Codes, so brand and generic cannot be told apart ([Liu 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6537862/)). Santos confirmed that Medicare paid for the transplant by requiring an inpatient transplant procedure claim within 14 days of the registry transplant date ([Santos 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4818663/)). The notes found no USRDS paper that publishes an algorithm combining Part B immunosuppressant claims with Part D records. **Inference:** methenamine, TMP-SMX, nitrofurantoin, cephalexin and fluoroquinolones are Part D drugs no matter who paid for the transplant. Immunosuppressant covariates in the plan's §3.2 L7 need Part B supplier claims for Medicare-paid transplants and Part D for others. **[Unverified]** Specific HCPCS (Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System) codes for tacrolimus, mycophenolate and belatacept come from background knowledge only.

## Part D transplant cohorts are small, poor and older

Requiring A+B+D shrinks and reshapes the cohort. Santos kept 21,117 of 83,309 adult transplants (about 25%) after requiring coverage, a Medicare-paid transplant claim and at least 180 days uncensored ([Santos 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4818663/)). About 60% of Abbott's cohort was dual-eligible, and the authors noted that dual eligibles receive Part D automatically ([Abbott 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5962376/)). Part D enrollees with immunosuppressant fills were older and further from transplant ([Liu 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6537862/)). Transplant Part D enrollment rose from 59.0% in 2011 to 69.7% in 2015, the lowest of the kidney failure groups ([USRDS ADR 2017](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/-/media/Files/USRDS/2017--Previous-ADR/ESRD/v2_c10_partd_17.pdf)). More than 20% of transplant patients had no known drug coverage, many of them employed ([USRDS ADR 2013](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/-/media/Files/USRDS/2013-Previous-ADR/Volume-2/Chapters/v2_ch6_13.pdf)). In general Medicare, a continuous-enrollment rule excluded 40.5% of SEER-Medicare patients, and those excluded were younger, less often white and more often died ([Papageorge 2022, General Medicare](https://doi.org/10.1111/jgs.18017)).

Every precedent study names generalizability as a limitation. None reweights to a wider population. **Inference:** USRDS registry variables (from the CMS-2728 form and transplant records) exist for nearly all recipients regardless of Part D. That would allow inverse-odds weights to carry results from the observable cohort to all recipients. This would be new; no source shows it done in USRDS. After 2021, any such target population must also account for the large MA share, which has no released claims.

## Conclusion

The literature gives the plan a floor, not a ceiling. Requiring continuous A, B and D with Medicare as primary payer and then censoring at coverage loss is universal. Weighting for that censoring is nearly absent, so the plan's IPCW would be a genuine methodological step for USRDS transplant research. But weights only work where some comparable patients stay observed. The two largest coverage shocks in this population break that condition. The 36-month cliff is fully determined for ESRD-only recipients, and the 2021 MA shift happened almost at once. The most useful change, then, is to define the target population around who can be observed, and to show it with observability curves. Building better weights for people who cannot be observed will not help.

The deeper gap is measurement, not modeling. No one has measured how well Part D captures short, cheap antibiotic courses in transplant recipients. Yet this trial's eligibility, exposure and outcome all rest on those fills. A simple check would turn an unstated assumption into a reported number: the fill-after-diagnosis rate by LIS status and by veteran status. That number would be worth reporting on its own.
